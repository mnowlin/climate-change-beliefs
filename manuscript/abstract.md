# abstract

Climate change is one of the most polarized issues among the US public. Previous research has identified several factors that influence the public's views on climate change, including cultural worldviews, political beliefs, environmental orientation, views on the scientific consensus, and solution aversion (i.e., opposition to climate policy solutions). Beliefs about climate change are interconnected and form a belief system. Recent work has noted that the interconnected beliefs in a belief system can be understood as a network. In a belief system network, beliefs are the nodes, edges represent correlations between beliefs, and the focus is on the centrality of beliefs within the network. Using a quota-based sample of the US public, I explore the belief system network associated with climate change, estimating both an undirected network (a Gaussian graphical model) and a directed Bayesian network over the same beliefs. Examining the major network centrality measures -- betweenness, closeness, and strength -- alongside the Bayesian network's out-degree and Markov blanket measures, I find that structural importance cuts across the deep core, policy core, and secondary belief hierarchy that theory predicts: individualism, perceived climate risk, and support for an international agreement are robustly central regardless of whether the network is modeled as undirected or directed, while egalitarianism's apparent centrality in the undirected model does not hold up once edge direction is estimated. These findings suggest that a belief's structural importance depends on both the type of belief and the modeling assumptions used to estimate it, complicating any single account -- hierarchical, gateway, or solution-aversion -- of how climate change beliefs are organized.
